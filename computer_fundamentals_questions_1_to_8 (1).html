QUESTION 1 · REMEMBERING

Four characteristics of modern digital computers and five generations

Four primary characteristics

  1. Speed: Executes millions or billions of operations per second.
  2. Accuracy: Produces precise results when the data and instructions are correct.
  3. Storage: Stores large amounts of data and retrieves it quickly.
  4. Automation and versatility: Automatically follows stored instructions and performs many kinds of tasks.

Hardware technology across five generations

GenerationApproximate periodKey hardware technologyExamples / features
First1940s–1950sVacuum tubesVery large, hot, power-hungry computers; machine language.
Second1950s–1960sTransistorsSmaller, faster and more reliable than tube-based machines.
Third1960s–1970sIntegrated circuits (ICs)Many components placed on a single chip.
Fourth1970s onwardMicroprocessors using VLSIPersonal computers and compact, affordable systems.
FifthCurrent and evolvingULSI, parallel processing and AI-oriented hardwareEmphasis on artificial intelligence, natural-language processing and advanced computing.
Generation dates can overlap, and fifth-generation descriptions vary by textbook. The key point is the progression from vacuum tubes to transistors, ICs, microprocessors, and increasingly advanced parallel/AI systems.
QUESTION 2 · UNDERSTANDING

Flow of data, instructions and control signals in a computer

A computer system combines the CPU, main memory and input/output (I/O) interfaces. They communicate through buses and control signals.

I/O interface
↔
CPU

↔
Main memory
Output devices

How the parts interact

  1. Input: A keyboard, scanner or other input device sends data through an I/O interface. The interface converts signals into a form the computer can use.
  2. Storage: Data and program instructions are stored in main memory (RAM) while the program runs.
  3. Fetch: The CPU control unit fetches an instruction from memory using the address held in the program counter.
  4. Decode: The control unit interprets the instruction and determines which operation and resources are required.
  5. Execute: The ALU performs arithmetic or logic operations, while registers temporarily hold instructions, addresses and data.
  6. Write back: Results are written to registers or memory, or sent through an I/O interface to an output device.

Three types of bus

  • Data bus: Carries the actual data and instruction values.
  • Address bus: Identifies the memory location or I/O register being accessed.
  • Control bus: Carries signals such as read, write, interrupt, clock and status signals.
Important: Data and addresses move through their respective paths, while the control unit coordinates operations by issuing control signals. Interrupts allow I/O devices to request CPU attention.
QUESTION 3 · APPLYING

Optical scanners and electronic card readers on a campus

A. Automating student attendance

  1. Give each student an ID card with a printed barcode or an RFID/smart-card chip.
  2. At a classroom entrance, the student scans the barcode or taps the card on a reader.
  3. The reader sends the student ID, date and time to an attendance application.
  4. The system checks the timetable and records attendance in a central database.
  5. Lecturers and administrators view reports and identify missing or duplicate records.

B. Automating library checkouts

  1. Place a barcode or RFID tag on each book and register it in the library database.
  2. The borrower scans or taps their student card to identify their account.
  3. The book is scanned; the system checks availability and creates a loan record with a due date.
  4. At return, the book is scanned again and its status is updated. Overdue reminders can be generated automatically.
TechnologyHow it worksCampus useConsiderations
Barcode scannerOptically reads printed bars and spaces.Attendance IDs and book checkout.Requires a visible, undamaged label and usually a direct scan.
OMR scannerDetects marked positions on special forms.Paper attendance sheets or surveys.Forms and marks must meet scanning requirements.
RFID readerCommunicates wirelessly with a tag.Quick card taps, book tracking and self-checkout.Read range, interference and unauthorized reads must be managed.
Smart-card readerReads data from a chip, often with contact or near-field communication.Student identity and access control.Requires compatible cards, readers and secure authentication.

Benefits and safeguards

  • Reduces manual data entry, queues and transcription errors.
  • Provides faster reports and a searchable record of transactions.
  • Use role-based access, encryption, backups and audit logs.
  • Protect student privacy, provide a manual fallback and prevent card sharing or duplicate scans.
QUESTION 4 · ANALYZING

Low-level and high-level programming languages

QUESTION 5 · ANALYZING

Mouse and trackball compared with digitizers and drawing tablets

FeatureMouseTrackballGraphics digitizer / drawing tablet
MechanismMove the whole device across a surface; optical sensor measures movement.Rotate a stationary ball with fingers or thumb; sensors measure ball movement.Stylus movement or position is detected across a digitizing surface; some tablets use absolute coordinates.
Coordinate trackingUsually relative: pointer moves based on device movement.Usually relative: ball rotation moves the pointer.Often absolute: a point on the tablet maps to a corresponding screen or drawing coordinate.
PrecisionGood for general selection and many CAD tasks.Useful for controlled cursor movement in limited desk space.High positional precision, useful for tracing plans and detailed illustration.
Pressure sensitivityGenerally not available.Generally not available.Many pen tablets detect pressure; some also detect tilt and pen angle.
Best fitGeneral desktop use and routine CAD selection.Workstations with limited movement space or users who prefer a stationary device.Digital painting, handwriting, tracing, drafting and detailed design input.

Application comparison

  • CAD: A mouse is convenient for menus and selecting objects; a digitizer can be better for tracing paper plans and entering precise points. Accuracy also depends on calibration, software and user technique.
  • Digital art: A pressure-sensitive pen tablet supports varied line width and brush opacity, producing a more natural drawing experience than a standard mouse or trackball.
A digitizer is not always more accurate in every workflow; choose based on required precision, coordinate mapping, comfort, software support and budget.
QUESTION 6 · EVALUATING

Drum/flatbed plotter versus UHD monitor and large-format printer

For most modern civil engineering offices, a high-resolution monitor paired with a large-format printer is the more practical general-purpose solution. A specialized plotter may still be justified for particular workflows or legacy equipment.

CriterionDrum / flatbed plotterUHD monitor + large-format printer
Line resolutionTraditional pen plotters can produce precise vector lines; output depends on mechanics, pen and media.UHD display helps inspect fine detail on screen; printer resolution, line control and media determine final paper quality.
ScalabilityLimited by machine size, media handling and supported formats.Digital drawings can be enlarged or revised easily; large-format printers support many sheet sizes and common CAD workflows.
VersatilityExcellent for certain vector drawing tasks but less versatile for mixed graphics and modern workflows.Supports CAD review, 3D visualization, mark-up, colour graphics and printed plans.
Initial costSpecialized plotters may require a significant investment and dedicated space.Cost varies widely; monitors and printers can be selected separately for the required workload.
Long-term costMay involve specialized maintenance, parts and supplies.Includes ink/toner, paper, maintenance and eventual hardware replacement; common supplies may be easier to source.

Recommendation

Choose a quality UHD monitor and large-format printer for most firms because it combines on-screen inspection, easy revision, broad file compatibility and convenient production of full-size plans. Before printing, verify scale, line weights, dimensions and paper size. Choose a dedicated plotter only when its precision, throughput, media support or integration provides a clear advantage.

Important distinction: A monitor does not replace a physical drawing. The printer produces the blueprint, so final line clarity and scale should be checked on a test print.
QUESTION 7 · EVALUATING

Facial recognition versus voice recognition for biometric authentication

In a noisy, publicly exposed environment, neither facial nor voice recognition should be treated as infallible. Performance depends on sensor quality, environmental conditions, anti-spoofing controls and implementation.

FactorFacial recognitionVoice recognition
Environmental reliabilityCan be affected by poor lighting, camera angle, masks, occlusion and image quality.Can be seriously affected by ambient noise, reverberation, distance and overlapping speech.
Public exposureA camera may capture a face without deliberate cooperation, creating privacy concerns.Nearby people may hear or record spoken prompts; speech can be replayed if the system is poorly designed.
Spoofing risksPhotos, videos or masks may attack weak systems; robust liveness checks reduce but do not eliminate risk.Recordings, synthesized speech or voice conversion may attack weak systems; challenge-response and anti-spoofing help.
AccessibilityMay be difficult for users with certain facial differences or when the face is covered.May be difficult for people with speech differences or in environments where speaking is unsuitable.
Privacy and biasRaises concerns about surveillance, demographic performance differences and retention of face data.Voice data can reveal identity and may contain sensitive information; performance can vary with accents and speech patterns.

Critical assessment and safeguards

  • High ambient noise makes voice recognition less reliable unless microphones and noise reduction are suitable.
  • Facial recognition may work without speech, but lighting, camera position and crowd conditions still matter.
  • Use a second factor such as a secure card or device-based authenticator for high-risk access.
  • Apply liveness detection, rate limits, encryption, minimal data retention, audit logs and a secure fallback process.
  • Test false acceptance and false rejection rates in the actual environment and across user groups.
Conclusion: In high-noise public spaces, face recognition may be more usable than voice recognition in some situations, but it is not automatically more secure. A well-designed multi-factor system with privacy protections is preferable to relying on either biometric alone.
QUESTION 8 · CREATING

Accessible, hands-free workstation for a visually impaired user

The workstation should let the user operate a computer and receive information without relying on sight or requiring routine hand use. The exact combination should be adapted to the user's abilities and preferences.

Proposed integrated system

ComponentPurposeHow it integrates
Voice recognition / speech controlHands-free commands, dictation and navigation.A microphone sends speech to accessibility software that converts supported commands into computer actions.
Screen readerReads interface text, menus, documents and status messages aloud.Works with the operating system and accessible applications; keyboard focus and labels must be properly exposed.
Speech synthesizer and headphonesProvides spoken feedback privately and clearly.Announces focus, errors, confirmations and document content; headphones reduce disturbance to others.
Refreshable Braille displayProvides tactile access to text and navigation.Connects to the screen reader and converts supported text into Braille cells.
Tactile keyboard / alternative switchBackup for precise shortcuts and situations where speech is unsuitable.Raised key markers or an accessible layout support touch typing; switches can be configured for individual needs.
Accessible scanner with OCRConverts printed documents into searchable text.The scanner captures a page; OCR recognizes text, which the screen reader reads aloud or sends to Braille.
Accessible setupImproves comfort, safety and independence.Use clear cable management, reachable controls, consistent device placement, adjustable volume and backup power.

Example workflow

  1. The user says a wake phrase or activates voice control and dictates a document.
  2. The screen reader announces the active application, menu and typing status.
  3. The user navigates with spoken commands or keyboard shortcuts and hears confirmation before important actions.
  4. For detailed reading, the user sends text to a refreshable Braille display.
  5. To read a paper handout, the user scans it, runs OCR and listens to the recognized text.

Safety, privacy and usability

  • Require confirmation before deleting files, sending messages or making purchases.
  • Use a headset microphone, local speech processing where possible, and secure login to protect private information.
  • Provide an alternative to voice commands for noisy rooms or speech-recognition errors.
  • Choose software and hardware with the user, then train and test the complete workflow with their everyday tasks.
Design principle: Accessibility is strongest when input, feedback and recovery work together. Voice control enables hands-free operation, while screen-reader speech and Braille provide non-visual feedback and a tactile alternative.
